# Chapter 11: Fever Dreams

\[Wren's POV\]

The fever broke some time before dawn, and Wren came out of it clean.

That was the first wrong thing.

She had been ill twice in her life that she could remember, and both times the coming out had been worse than the going in. A week of being wrung out. Legs that would not hold.

This was not that.

She opened her eyes and the roof was in focus and stayed in focus.

The fire had burned down to a red bed. Tamsin was sitting beside it on a stool with her hands loose in her lap, awake, watching the door.

"You've been up all night."

Tamsin did not startle. "You've been talking all night."

"What did I say."

"You know what you said."

Wren pushed herself up onto one elbow and waited for the wound along her ribs to punish her for it.

It did not.

She stopped, halfway up, and stayed there.

"Tamsin."

"I know."

"It doesn't hurt."

"I know."

Wren got the rest of the way up. There was a tightness where the stitches were, the kind of pull you get from a scar that has had months to settle, but the deep sick throb that had been there yesterday was gone entirely.

She looked down at herself.

The dressing was off. Tamsin must have taken it off in the night and not replaced it, and Wren could see the line of stitches clearly in the firelight.

The skin on either side of the thread had closed. Not scabbed. Closed, pink and new and joined, with the black stitches sitting on top of it like something left behind on a finished job.

Fourteen stitches, holding shut a wound that no longer needed them.

"That was a day ago," Wren said.

"Fourteen hours."

"That's not."

"No," Tamsin said. "It isn't."

Wren touched the edge of it. The new skin gave under her finger and did not open.

Something went through her that she did not have a word for. It was not relief. It sat in her chest alongside the second pulse, and it was very cold, and it felt a great deal like the moment on the platform when the light had not faded.

"I want you to tell me I'm remembering it wrong," she said. "The size of it."

"You're not."

"Tell me anyway."

"No." Tamsin got up off the stool.

"I won't do that to you. You'll get enough of it from everyone else."

She crossed to the pallet, crouched, and took Wren's chin in her hand, turning her face toward the light with the impersonal competence of a woman who had done it ten thousand times.

"Look up. Now left."

"My head's fine."

"Your head took a blow that should have killed you on its own." Tamsin's thumb pulled down under one eye.

"And it's fine. That's not comfort, girl, that's the fourth thing this week that shouldn't be true."

She let go and sat back on her heels.

For a while neither of them said anything. The fire ticked. Outside, the wind had dropped, and the quiet that replaced it was worse.

"How fast," Wren said at last.

"How fast what."

"How fast is it supposed to be. For anyone else. If I'd stayed in Duskmoor and this had happened, how long."

Tamsin's mouth tightened.

"A wound that deep, in a healthy wolf, with no rot in it and someone competent stitching it. Three weeks before you'd walk easy. Six before you'd forget about it."

"And the skull."

"Two months, if you were lucky, and you'd never be quite the same after." She said it flatly.

"You were not lucky. You were left in the snow for most of a night first."

Wren sat with that.

She had never been strong. That was the thing that kept circling. She had been an unremarkable wolf from an unremarkable line, the sort who does not place in the youth trials and is not expected to, and she had been glad of it because glad was easier than the alternative.

Her whole life had been evidence that there was nothing in her.

And her whole life, underneath it, there had been a second pulse that never showed up when she counted.

"My mother knew," she said.

Tamsin looked at her sharply.

"She made me hide. Not the bond, not the Rite, everything. Stay small, stay quiet, don't be good at anything in front of anyone."

Wren's hands had gone tight in the blanket.

"I thought she was frightened of the pack. She wasn't, was she. She was frightened of this."

"I don't know what your mother was frightened of."

"You know what she should have been frightened of."

Tamsin stood up.

She went to the work table and began moving things on it, picking up a jar and setting it down in the same place, and Wren understood that she was watching a woman buy herself time.

"You should sleep," Tamsin said.

"I've slept."

"Then rest."

"Tamsin." Wren's voice came out steadier than she expected.

"You barred the door. You've been sitting up watching it all night. You know what the word means and you know what's under the brand and you've decided not to tell me, and I'm lying here healing at four times the speed of a person."

The jar came down harder than the last one.

"I'm not asking you to like me," Wren said. "I'm asking what I am."

Tamsin stood with her back turned and both hands flat on the table.

When she finally spoke, it was not to Wren, and it was not really an answer.

"If I send you back across that border," she said, "they'll finish what they started inside a week."

"I know."

"And if I let you walk out into the Wilds on your own, with that on your skin and that in your blood, you will not last a month, and it will not be the Sanctum that does it."

She turned around. Her face had the look of a woman at the end of an argument she had been having with herself since moonrise, and losing.

"There's no door I can put you out of," Tamsin said. "That's the trouble. I've been sitting here all night trying to find one."
