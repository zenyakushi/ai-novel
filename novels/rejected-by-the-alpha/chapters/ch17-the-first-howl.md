# Chapter 17: The First Howl (As They Tell It)

\[Wren's POV\]

Tamsin made her eat first, which Wren resented and did anyway.

"Now," Tamsin said, when the bowls were down. "Tell me the First Howl."

"What?"

"The story. The one they teach. Tell it to me."

"You said you'd explain."

"And I will, and I'm not starting in the middle." Tamsin settled back against the wall with her hands round a cup.

"Tell it properly. The way you'd tell a child."

Wren's jaw set. Something in her chest pulled back from it before she had worked out why.

Then she gave in, because there was nothing else to do.

"In the beginning the wolves had no way to find each other," she said.

"Go on."

"They lived and they died and they took mates by choice or by force or by chance, and most of them chose wrong, and the packs tore themselves apart over it for a hundred generations." Wren heard her own voice fall into the rhythm of it. The words had been put in her at four years old and had never had to be recalled since, because they had never once left.

"Aluna looked down and grieved. She had made a people who could not find the one they were made for."

"And so?"

"And so she opened her own throat and let out the First Howl. It went across the whole world and into every wolf alive, and where it touched two who belonged together it left a thread." Wren turned her cup.

"And from that day, at the moon's own rite, the thread would show itself, and no wolf need ever choose wrong again."

She stopped.

"That's it. That's the whole of it. There's a verse about the thread being the Goddess's own voice, still sounding, but I never learned the middle part properly."

"You learned it properly," Tamsin said. "Everyone learns it properly."

"Keep going. The last part."

"The last part isn't story, it's the doctrine."

"Say it anyway."

Wren said it.

"The bond is the gift. The bond is not given to be questioned. What the bond joins, the Rite reveals, and what the Rite reveals, the Sanctum keeps."

The fire moved.

"Good," Tamsin said quietly. "Now. How old is that story?"

"As old as the packs. Older."

"Who told you that?"

Wren opened her mouth and found nothing in it.

"The liturgy," she said, after a moment. "It says. In the liturgy."

"So the story says the story is old." Tamsin's eyebrows went up a fraction.

"Right. Second question. In that whole telling, how many times does a wolf do anything?"

"What?"

"Anything at all. Decide, refuse, ask a question. How many?"

Wren ran it back through her head.

The wolves chose wrong. The wolves tore themselves apart. Aluna grieved, Aluna howled, Aluna gave.

And then the wolves went to the Rite, forever, and were shown.

"None," she said slowly. "After the gift. Not one."

"Not one," Tamsin agreed. "Doesn't that strike you as convenient?"

"It's a story about a goddess. The goddess does things. That's what they're for."

"It is. And I want you to notice that this particular one ends with a rule about who keeps it." Tamsin drank.

"What the Rite reveals, the Sanctum keeps. That's not theology, girl. That's an administrative arrangement with a hymn round it."

Wren sat with that. Her throat had gone dry and she did not reach for the cup, because reaching for it would have been an answer of a kind.

She had heard the last line spoken over her head more times than she could count. At every Rite since she was small, in the same flat cadence, by a different officiant in the same red robes.

She had never once heard it as a claim. It had simply been the end of the words, the way the bottom of a cup is the end of the cup.

"That's not proof of anything," she said.

"No. It isn't."

"You're telling me the story's convenient. Convenient isn't false."

"No," Tamsin said, and she sounded almost pleased. "It isn't. You're not stupid, which is going to make the rest of tonight harder rather than easier."

Wren pushed on anyway, because something had started turning over and would not stop.

"The bond found me," she said. "That's the part I keep coming back to."

"Whatever else, the thing worked. It lit. It reached across eight hundred wolves and it found him and it held, and everybody saw it."

"It did."

"So the story's right about the one part that matters."

"Is it?" Tamsin set the cup down.

"Say the story's true. Aluna's own voice, still sounding, joining two who belong together. Then answer me one thing."

"Go on."

"Why can it be stopped?"

Wren's mouth opened.

"A goddess's gift," Tamsin said, "the one true thing, the thread that cannot choose wrong. And a woman in red robes raised her hand and halted it in front of eight hundred people, and it obeyed her."

"Yseult didn't stop the bond. She stopped the sealing. The bond had already..."

"The bond had already lit, and held, and been witnessed, and then a man read a sentence off a piece of paper and it came apart and burned a mark into you." Tamsin's voice did not rise at all. "Tell me what part of that is a goddess."

The fire made its small noise. Under Wren's breastbone the second pulse went on keeping its own time, and she was suddenly, furiously aware of it.

Wren looked down at her own hands around the cup, and then, without deciding to, at the neckline of her shirt, under which the black lines sat on her collarbone and did not catch the light.

She had asked herself a hundred questions since the platform. Why her, why him, why that night, what her mother had known.

She had never once asked why a divine gift had a procedure for cancellation.

"There's a paperwork to it," she said. "Isn't there."

Tamsin did not answer that directly.

She got up, slowly, the way she did at the end of a long day, and went to the fire and crouched to bank it, and stayed crouched with her back turned for a while.

"That's the story they let us keep," she said.
