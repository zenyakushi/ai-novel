# Chapter 9: The Healer's Hut

\[Wren's POV\]

Hands turned her over, and the pain came back all at once, which meant she was not as far gone as she had thought.

She got one eye open. Snow, and boots, and above the boots a heavy coat gone shapeless with age, and above that nothing she could resolve.

"Don't," Wren said. It came out as almost nothing.

"Don't what." A woman's voice, low and unbothered.

"Don't help, or don't touch."

"Don't. Report."

There was a pause at that.

"Well," the woman said. "That answers one thing."

Fingers found the side of Wren's throat and stayed there, counting. They were bare in the cold and they were steady, and there was something about being touched with that much competence that undid her more than the kindness would have.

"You've been out here half the night."

"Longer."

"No, half. I know what longer looks like." The fingers went away.

"You should not be talking. I want you to keep doing it anyway, so I know when you stop."

Something was pushed under Wren's shoulder and levered, and the world tipped, and the wound along her ribs woke all the way up and screamed.

She heard a sound come out of herself that she did not recognize as hers.

"That one's bad," the woman agreed, as if Wren had made an observation. "Keep talking."

"About what."

"Anything that isn't your name."

The dragging started then, and after that the pieces came apart again.

She had a piece of being hauled backward over snow with her heels ploughing two lines behind her. A piece of a low branch going past her face close enough to feel.

A piece of the woman stopping twice, breathing hard, swearing at nothing in particular, and starting again.

The woman was not young. Wren worked that out from the sound of the stopping rather than from anything she saw, and it arrived with a thin useless guilt she had no room to hold.

At some point the sky went away and there was a roof instead.

Not much of one. Turf and old timber, low enough that the woman had to stoop under it, and warm, and that was the part that hurt worst of all. Coming into warmth felt like being skinned.

"I know," the woman said. "I know. It passes."

Wren was on a pallet by then. There was a fire somewhere to her left, and a smell of wet wool drying too fast, and the woman's hands were working at the ruined shift along her ribs with a knife.

"Keep talking."

"You said not my name."

"I did."

"Then I've run out."

A short breath. Not quite a laugh.

"Count, then. Out loud. From one."

Wren counted. She got to nineteen and lost it and started again at one, and the woman did not correct her.

The knife went through the last of the shift and the cold air got at the wound and Wren's counting stopped.

"Up to forty-one," the woman said. "That's better than most."

Hands pressed. Something that stung past describing went into the wound and Wren's whole body tried to leave the pallet and did not manage it.

"You've a hole in you the width of my thumb and a skull that's taken a proper hit. The ribs I can close. The head I can only watch."

"Why."

"Why what."

"Why any of it."

The woman did not answer straight away. Wren heard her set something down, metal on wood.

"Because I found you," she said. "That's the whole of it. Don't make it into more."

The answer had come too quickly to be the true one. Wren let it stand. She was in no condition to pull at a thread that was currently the only thing holding her together.

"They'll come back."

"They won't. I've been out here nineteen years and they have never once come back to check."

A cloth, wrung out, laid across Wren's forehead. "That's the one mercy in how they do it. They cannot be bothered."

Wren turned that over and found it did not comfort her at all.

The light from the fire moved on the underside of the turf roof. She watched it and let the woman work and thought about how it had been a whole day, near enough, since anyone had put a hand on her that was not trying to move her somewhere she did not want to go.

"What do I call you," she said.

"Tamsin." A pause.

"And I know what they call you, so don't waste it. Word crosses the border faster than people do."

That should have frightened her. It was too much effort.

Tamsin worked. Wren drifted, came back, drifted. The fire cracked once, loudly, and she surfaced enough to notice that the second pulse was still going under everything, slower now, but keeping its own count the way it always had.

Tamsin was cleaning the skin above the wound, working upward, the cloth going in short careful passes toward Wren's collarbone.

Toward the brand.

"That one's fresh," Wren said. "You don't need to."

"I need to see all of it."

The cloth reached the mark. Tamsin wiped once, and then again, slower.

Then she stopped.

She did not move at all. Not her hand, not the cloth, nothing. The fire went on making its noise and the wind went on outside and Tamsin knelt there over Wren's chest with her hand gone completely still.

"What," Wren said.

Nothing.

"What is it."

Tamsin's voice, when it came, was not the voice she had been using all night. It had gone careful in a way that had nothing to do with the wound.

"How long have you had this."

"Since last night. You watched them do it. Everybody watched."

"Not the brand."

Tamsin's thumb pressed lightly at the skin just beneath the black lines, where the firelight caught, where Wren had never once thought to look.

"Under it," she said. "How long have you had what's under it."
