# Chapter 12: What the Wilds Keep

\[Wren's POV\]

Tamsin gave her a knife on the fourth day.

Not a good one. The handle had been rewrapped twice and the blade had been sharpened so often it had gone narrow, and she put it down on the blanket without ceremony.

"You'll cut yourself with it before you cut anything else."

"I know how to hold a knife."

"You know how to hold a kitchen knife." Tamsin was already turning away.

"Different grip. I'll show you when you can stand up without the wall."

Wren could stand without the wall. She had proved it twice that morning while Tamsin was outside, and had said nothing about it, and did not know yet whether that was caution or habit.

She put her hand on the knife and left it there.

"Tell me about out here," she said. "If I'm staying."

Tamsin stopped.

"That's the first useful thing you've asked me."

She came back and sat on the stool, and for the first time since the night of the fever she looked at Wren directly and did not look away from her again.

"Right. Rules of the Wilds. There are four and nobody wrote them down."

"Go on."

"One. Nobody out here asks what you did." Tamsin held up a finger.

"Not ever. Half of them were branded for something and half of them were branded for nothing, and there's no way to tell which by looking, so you don't ask and they don't ask you."

"And if someone does ask?"

"Then they want something from you, and you should already be walking."

Wren filed that.

"Two. Nobody owes you shelter, but most will trade for it." A second finger.

"Food, salt, thread, news. News is worth more than food some winters. If someone gives you a thing for nothing, that's not kindness, that's a debt you didn't agree to."

"You dragged me nine miles and stitched me up for nothing."

Tamsin's expression did not move. "Did I."

Wren let that sit, and did not like the shape of it.

"Three," Tamsin went on. "The border is not a wall, it's a habit. Nobody patrols it."

"What crosses it is people who want something on the other side, and that runs both directions, and it is the single most dangerous place out here."

"Because of the enforcers."

"Because of everyone. Exiles who've decided to go back and settle something."

"Pack scouts who've decided the Wilds are worth a look. Traders who are neither." Tamsin's mouth flattened.

"The border is where people are making decisions. Stay off it."

"And four?"

"Four is the only one that matters." Tamsin leaned forward with her elbows on her knees. "The Wilds do not keep anyone who makes themselves interesting."

Wren felt that land somewhere under her ribs, next to the second pulse.

"Everyone out here is hiding," Tamsin said. "From a pack, from a sentence, from a name."

"And it works. It works because the Sanctum has better things to do than count corpses in a place they've already written off."

"Until someone's worth counting."

"Until someone's worth counting."

The fire moved. Wren looked at the three bars on the door and understood them properly for the first time.

"Who else is out here?" she asked.

"Actually out here. Not in general."

Tamsin considered her for a moment, apparently deciding how much was safe.

"There's a family four hours north with two children and a goat, and they've been there eleven years and they have never once come to me for anything, which tells you what they think of company."

"That's one."

"There's a man who traps east of the split rock. He talks to himself and he's harmless and he'll give you a rabbit if you give him thread." Tamsin counted on her fingers.

"There are the Vigil, further out, and you won't meet them and you shouldn't want to."

"Why not."

"Because they're the only ones out here who are organized." Tamsin's eyes came up.

"Organized people have plans. Plans need pieces."

Wren turned the knife over on the blanket.

"And the rest?"

"The rest are rogues, and there are more of them every year, and they hunt the border in threes and fours because a rogue on his own doesn't last a winter." Tamsin stood and began putting things away with more care than the task required.

"That's your lot. That's everyone between here and the mountains."

It was not a comforting list. It was, Wren realized, the first complete answer anyone had given her since the platform.

"Thank you," she said.

Tamsin made a small dismissive sound and did not turn around. But her shoulders came down a fraction, and she did not answer, and somehow that was better than if she had.

They ate. Wren managed the whole bowl and most of a second, and afterward she sat with the blanket round her shoulders and let the warmth get into her properly for the first time in four days.

She had spent nineteen years in a pack that had shut its doors in one night.

She had spent four days with a woman who would not tell her anything and who had also not once let her go hungry, cold, or unattended, and who had given her a knife.

Wren did not trust her. She was beginning to trust that she could survive her, which was not the same thing and was more than she had anywhere else.

Tamsin was banking the fire when she stopped, half bent over it, with the poker still in her hand.

"Quiet," she said.

"I didn't say any..."

"Quiet."

Wren went quiet.

At first she heard nothing. The wind had dropped again. The fire settled, and there was the small tick of the cooling pot, and beyond that the enormous silence of a country with nobody in it.

Then, a long way off, a howl.

It went up and held and fell away, and another answered it, further west.

Wren's shoulders came down. "They're wolves. There are wolves out here, you said so, you said..."

"Listen to the end of it."

A third went up.

It climbed, and held, and then it stopped. Cut clean, all at once, on a note it had not finished.

No wolf ended a call like that. A wolf ran out of breath and trailed. This had been shut off, the way a man stops a sound when he has heard the answer he was listening for.

Tamsin set the poker down very quietly.

"That's not wolves," she said. "Get your boots."
