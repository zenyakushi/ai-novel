# Chapter 19: A Second Wolf

\[Wren's POV\]

She did not sleep, and in the morning Tamsin was waiting for her with her coat already on.

"Outside."

"It's barely light."

"That's the point. Bring the knife."

They walked forty minutes north, past the gully, up onto open ground where the trees thinned to nothing and the snow lay unbroken in every direction.

Tamsin stopped and turned a slow circle, looking at the horizon rather than the ground.

"Why here?"

"Because I want to see anyone coming for a long time before they arrive." Tamsin sat down on a rock. "And because whatever you do, I'd rather you didn't do it in my house."

Wren's stomach turned over.

"You want me to try."

"I want you to find out. There's a difference and you'll understand it in a minute."

"You spent all last night telling me people have been killed for four hundred years for having this."

"I did."

"And now you want me to go looking for it."

"Yes." Tamsin's face did not soften.

"Because they are going to come for you whether you can do anything or not. That was decided in a register before you could walk. The only part still open is whether you're any use to yourself when they arrive."

Wren stood in the snow with her hands at her sides.

"How," she said.

"I don't know."

"That's not..."

"I have never had it and I have never seen it and the woman who told me was dead before she got that far." Tamsin folded her arms.

"I can tell you what I'd do. I'd start with the part of you that's already been doing it without asking."

Wren knew exactly what she meant.

She closed her eyes.

It was there. It was always there, under the breastbone, slower than her heart, keeping its own count in a room nobody else could hear.

She had spent nineteen years building a wall between herself and it. Pressing her fingers to her wrist and finding one rhythm and being glad. Not listening, deliberately, every day, until not listening had become a muscle.

Taking the wall down was harder than she expected.

She stood in the cold and reached toward the thing in her chest, and it did not move, and she could feel her own effort bouncing off something that was not resisting her so much as failing to notice her.

Minutes went by. Her feet went numb.

"Nothing," she said.

"Keep going."

"I don't know what I'm reaching *with*."

"Neither did anybody, the first time." Tamsin had not moved from the rock.

"Sit down. Standing up you're bracing. You brace like you're waiting to be hit."

Wren sat down in the snow.

She stopped trying to grab at it. She let her hands rest on her knees and she breathed, and she stopped commanding and started listening, and that was different enough that she noticed the difference immediately.

The second pulse went on under her breastbone.

She followed it.

Not toward it. Along it, following where it ran rather than pushing at where it sat.

And somewhere past the place she had always stopped, the corridor kept going.

Wren's breath caught.

There was more of it. That was the whole of what she understood in that moment, and it went through her like cold water. The pulse was not a thing in her chest.

The pulse was the near end of something, and the something went back and down and away in a direction her body did not have a word for.

*What are you.*

This time, it heard her.

She felt it turn.

Nothing moved in the snow. Nothing lit up, nothing burned, no thread came out of her chest. What happened was smaller than that and much worse: for one half of one second, something enormous and patient that had been asleep her entire life opened one eye and looked back.

Wren made a sound and went over sideways into the snow.

"Wren."

Tamsin was off the rock and down beside her. Wren got a hand up to stop her.

"I'm fine. I'm fine, don't."

"What happened."

"It answered."

She was shaking. She noticed it distantly and could not stop it, and the odd thing, the thing she would come back to later, was that she was not frightened.

She was exhilarated, and underneath that, hungry.

"It's not a pulse," Wren said. "It was never a pulse. That's just the bit that fits inside me."

Tamsin sat back on her heels in the snow. Her face had gone very carefully blank.

"How much is there."

"I don't know." Wren got herself upright. Her hands would not be still.

"A lot. Tamsin, there's a lot."

"All right."

"I want to do it again."

"No."

"I nearly had it..."

"I said no." Tamsin's voice cracked across the open ground harder than Wren had ever heard it.

"Look at your hands. Look at them. You reached at it for four seconds and it put you on the ground."

Wren looked at her hands. They were shaking hard enough to be embarrassing.

"You are going to want more of that," Tamsin said, quieter. "I've watched people want things before. I'd rather you knew it was happening while it's happening."

Wren said nothing, because she could not honestly disagree.

They walked back. She was clumsy on the slope in a way she had not been on the way out, and Tamsin took her elbow twice and did not comment.

At the gully Tamsin stopped.

She stood with her head slightly turned. She had stood exactly so on the night of the howls.

"What," Wren said.

"Nothing." Tamsin's eyes went along the tree line and came back.

"Nothing. Come on."

They went down toward the hut, and Wren got twenty paces before she looked back.

She did not know why. Something had put it into her, the same way she had known to turn into the hand on her ankle instead of away from it.

Four hundred paces up the ridge, on ground they had not crossed and nobody had crossed all winter, the snow was broken.

One line of prints. They came in flat along the rock from the east, stopped, lay in a long disturbed patch where a body had waited a while, and went out again the other way.

They had not been there on the walk up. She had looked at that ridge for forty minutes while she sat in the snow failing to reach the thing in her chest, and there had been nothing on it at all.

Whatever had opened its eye out there, she had not been the only one who felt it.
