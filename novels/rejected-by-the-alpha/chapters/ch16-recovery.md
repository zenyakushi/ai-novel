# Chapter 16: Recovery

\[Wren's POV\]

They went back to the hut after three days on the shelf, and nothing had been taken and nothing had been broken, and somehow that was worse.

"They didn't even turn it over," Wren said.

"Why would they." Tamsin was already rebuilding the fire. "There's nothing in here worth the bending down."

By the end of the second week Wren could carry water.

By the end of the third she could split wood, badly, and then less badly, and then well enough that Tamsin stopped watching her do it.

The stitches came out on the sixteenth day. There was a pale seam where the wound had been, thin as a thread, already fading at the ends.

Tamsin looked at it a long time and said nothing, and Wren had learned by then not to fill that kind of silence.

They settled into a shape. Tamsin gathered and sorted and ground and did not explain what any of it was for. Wren fetched, carried, cut, and asked one question a day, which was the rate Tamsin would tolerate before she stopped answering altogether.

Wren chose them carefully.

*How do you know when the ground's about to give.* Answered.

*What do you trade with the trapper.* Answered.

*Why three bars on the door.* Not answered.

On the twenty-second day, she used her question on something else.

They were outside. The light was going and the cold had come up out of the ground the way it did every evening, and Tamsin was cutting back a stand of something woody that Wren had not learned the name of yet.

"You said nineteen years," Wren said.

Tamsin's blade did not stop. "I did."

"Nineteen years is a strange number to be exact about."

"It's the number."

"You knew it without counting." Wren set down the bundle she was carrying.

"People who've been somewhere a long time say twenty. They round. You didn't round."

Tamsin cut two more stems. Then she straightened up, and put the blade down on the stump, and Wren understood that something had just been decided.

"Sit down," Tamsin said.

Wren sat on the stump.

"I was a pack healer," Tamsin said. "Not out here. A proper one, in a proper hall, with an apprentice and a stipend and a name people knew for forty miles."

"Which pack?"

"That's your second question and you've had your one."

"Then I'll spend tomorrow's."

Something that was almost amusement crossed the old woman's face and did not stay.

"There was a Rite," Tamsin said. "Nineteen years ago. A girl about your age lit up for an Alpha's son, and the Oracle stopped it, and it was declared unfit, and she was branded in front of her own pack."

Something went down through Wren's chest, cold and slow, and settled next to the second pulse.

"They marched her to the border the same night. Same law. Same words, near enough, I'd guess."

Tamsin's voice had gone flat and careful. "There was an instruction attached to it. It didn't go in the decree."

"It went round the healers' hall quietly, from someone who outranked all of us."

"What was the instruction."

"That she was to be left. That if she came back across, or if anyone found her, there was to be no treatment given, no shelter offered, and no report made of either." Tamsin's hands had gone still at her sides.

"In writing it would have been a crime. Spoken, it was just a thing everybody had heard."

"And you treated her."

"She crawled back to the border ditch four days later." Tamsin said it without inflection. "She was nineteen years old and she had walked four days on a brand that had gone septic, and she got as far as the ditch below the healers' hall, and I could hear her from the window."

Wren did not say anything.

"I went down," Tamsin said. "I brought her in. I treated her for two days in a store room with the door shut and I did not report it."

"Did she live?"

"No."

The word sat there in the cold between them. Wren found she had stopped breathing and had to start again deliberately.

"She died on the third morning and I have never once been able to decide whether I did that for her or for me." Tamsin picked up the blade again and looked at it without using it.

"They came for me before she was cold. There wasn't a trial. There wasn't anything."

"There was a man from the Spire reading out that I had obstructed a lawful disposition, and then there was the road, and then there was out here."

"They branded you."

"They did."

Wren looked at the woman's collar, which was buttoned, and had always been buttoned, in a hut where nobody else ever came.

"Nineteen years," Wren said slowly.

"Nineteen years."

"I'm nineteen."

Tamsin's eyes came up.

"Yes," she said. "I'd worked that out."

Wren sat on the stump with her hands on her knees while the light went, and turned it over, and could not make it lie flat.

A girl branded nineteen years ago for a bond that lit too bright. A healer who had been out here exactly as long as Wren had been alive.

Two things, nineteen years apart from nothing, and her chest had gone tight around the pair of them.

"It isn't a coincidence," she said. "Is it."

"I don't know," Tamsin said, and it was the first answer all night that sounded like it cost her nothing. "That's the honest one. I have had nineteen years to decide and I don't know."

"But you've got a guess."

"I've got more than a guess. I've got the shape of a thing I was told once and didn't believe." Tamsin slid the blade into her belt.

"And I'm not giving it to you tonight."

"Tamsin."

"No." She said it without heat. "You've had one question and I've given you my whole life, and I'm tired, and what's left isn't the sort of thing you tell somebody at the end of a cold day when they're sitting on a stump."

She started back toward the hut. At the door she stopped, with her hand on the topmost bar, the way she always did.

"Tomorrow," Tamsin said, without turning round. "Sit down properly and eat something first. You'll want to be sitting down."
