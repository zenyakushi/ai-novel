# Chapter 7: Filed as Handled

\[Veyra's POV\]

The morning's returns came up from the lower registry at the sixth bell, as they had every morning for thirty-one years.

Archon Malrick Veyra did not read them at his desk. He read them standing at the window slit, where the light came in at an angle that made poor penmanship obvious.

Eleven filings. A grain levy dispute out of Fenmoor, two border infractions, a death in custody at Greywatch with a healer's mark against it.

And one Rite disposition out of Duskmoor, filed at dawn, closed the same hour it was opened.

He read that one twice.

The hand was the enforcer captain's, which he recognized without needing the seal. Heavy downstrokes. A man who pressed too hard and thought it looked decisive.

*Subject conveyed past the Wilds boundary in accordance with the Exile Law. Subject did not survive the night. Cause: exposure and the Wilds. File closed.*

Veyra set the sheet on the sill and looked at it a while longer.

"Sound," he said.

The clerk at the door straightened. He was new enough to still be standing when he could have been sitting. "Archon."

"How long is the Duskmoor border road, from the outer gate to the last checkpoint?"

"I would have to check the survey, Archon."

"Nine miles. It was nine miles when I walked it and it has not moved."

Veyra turned the sheet over, found the back blank, and turned it again. "How long does nine miles take, in snow, on foot, with four men walking a barefoot girl?"

The clerk did not answer. It had not been the sort of question that wanted one.

"They left the gate an hour past midnight. This is filed at dawn." He held the sheet up to the light.

"They walked her nine miles, watched her die of exposure, and were back through the gate in time to write it up. All in one night."

"Perhaps they did not wait, Archon."

"No," Veyra said. "Perhaps they did not."

He crossed to the case behind his desk and drew out the bound registry, the wide one, the one whose spine had been rebound twice since his appointment and whose pages went back further than that.

He did not need the index. His thumb found the section the way it always did.

The Bloodline Registry did not list many names anymore. That was the point of it. Four generations of careful work had reduced a problem to a short column, and a short column to a habit, and a habit to eleven filings a morning that a clerk could carry up the stairs.

He ran a finger down the column and stopped.

*Ashveil, W. Duskmoor. Maternal line unverified. Flagged pending Rite.*

The flag was his own, set nineteen years ago in ink that had gone brown at the edges. He had set four hundred and six such flags in his career. Three had ever mattered.

He read the entry, and the enforcer's filing beside it, and the two documents did not sit well together in his hands.

"Archon?"

"The Rite," Veyra said. "What did the girl's bond do."

"The report from the Oracle says it ignited, Archon. Fully. In front of the amphitheater."

"It held."

"It held," the clerk agreed, "until it was severed."

Veyra nodded slowly. Nothing in his face moved.

A common bond flickers and dies. He had watched it happen perhaps two thousand times from the Spire's gallery, and could have told you the exact shade of the light and how long it lasted, which was not long.

A bond that ignites, and holds, and has to be cut, is not a common bond. It is an event. And an event is followed by a body, and a body is followed by paperwork, and the paperwork had arrived before dawn with heavy downstrokes and no detail in it at all.

"Did anyone else see her after the gate?"

"The file says four enforcers, Archon."

"The file says what four men agreed to say." He closed the registry, and the sound of it was very small in the room. "That is not the same thing, and it never has been."

He sat down then, at last, and took a fresh sheet and did not write on it immediately.

There were two ways to be wrong about a thing like this. A man could believe a report he should have doubted, and a man could tear down a wall to find nothing behind it and be seen doing it. Of the two, he had always considered the first the more expensive, and the second only embarrassing.

He had outlived a great many men who had ranked those the other way around.

Something moved in his chest that he had not felt in years and did not care for. He waited for it to pass, and it did, and he made a note of how long it took.

"Sound. The enforcer captain and his men."

"Archon?"

"Commend them." He wrote three lines and did not sign them yet.

"The filing was prompt and the language was correct. Say so in writing, and see that the commendation is read aloud where the other companies will hear it."

The clerk's pen scratched. "Yes, Archon."

"They will be pleased," Veyra said. "Men who have done a thing badly are always pleased to be told they did it well. It settles them."

He set the commendation aside and took a second sheet, smaller, of a paper the lower registry did not stock.

This one he wrote slowly, and the hand he used was not the hand he used for filings.

"Is the tracker Ilsa still quartered at the east stair?"

The clerk's pen stopped. "I believe so, Archon."

"Send her up. Not through the registry floor. The east stair, and not before the bell."

He folded the small sheet twice and pressed his ring into the wax without warming it first, which left the mark shallow and hard to read, which was the point.

"And Sound. The Duskmoor file."

"Archon?"

"Leave it closed." Veyra turned back to the window, where the light was going flat as the sun climbed past the angle that made bad penmanship obvious.

"Closed files are not reviewed. I would rather nobody thought to review this one."
