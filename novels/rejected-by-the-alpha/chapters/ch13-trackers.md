# Chapter 13: Trackers

\[Wren's POV\]

They did not run. That was the part Wren had not expected.

Tamsin moved through the hut at a walk, and everything she picked up went into the same pack in the same order, and Wren understood that she was watching a thing that had been rehearsed.

"How many times have you done this?"

"Four." Tamsin dropped the stoppered jars into a roll of cloth. "Twice for nothing."

"And the other two?"

"Put your boots on."

Wren put her boots on. They were Tamsin's, too wide, stuffed at the toe with rag.

Outside, the cold took her breath for a second. The night had gone still and enormous, and the snow had a crust on it that broke under the first step and then held.

Tamsin took the fire apart before she left. Not banked. Apart, scraped out, the ashes carried in a pan and flung wide across open ground where the wind would take them.

"Cold hearth," she said, seeing Wren watching. "Warm one tells them how long ago. Cold one tells them nothing."

They went uphill, and east, and then north along a line that was not a path.

Tamsin walked it without hesitating, and never once looked back at the hut, and Wren, who had been shown exactly one way to leave a place in her life and it had involved four men and a decree, found she had a great deal to learn.

After a time they came onto a shelf of rock above a gully, and Tamsin stopped and crouched and put a hand out flat, and Wren crouched beside her.

From here the hut was a dark shape a long way down, half under the ridge.

"Now we wait."

"We're not going further?"

"No. Going further is how you get found." Tamsin's voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"Whoever it is, they'll come to the hut. We watch what they do there, and then we know what they are, and then we decide."

"And if they come up here?"

"Then I was wrong, and you run north and don't stop."

Wren looked at her.

"That's the whole plan?"

"That's the whole plan." Tamsin's eyes stayed on the hut. "Plans out here are short."

They waited a long time.

The cold got into Wren by degrees, through the boots first, then the knees, then everywhere. She kept her hands inside her sleeves and did not complain, and beneath everything the second pulse went on doing what it always did, steady and unbothered, and she noticed for the first time that she was cold but not suffering.

Fourteen hours to close a wound. Four days from the snow to a hillside in the dark, and her body was keeping up.

She thought about the enforcer's file. She had never seen it and could imagine it exactly. *Subject did not survive the night.*

"Tamsin."

"Quiet."

"One question."

A pause. "One."

"When they exile someone. Is it meant to work?"

Tamsin did not answer immediately, and when she did her voice had something old in it.

"No."

"Then the Wilds aren't a sentence. They're a method."

"Took me six years to work that out," Tamsin said. "You've had four days. Quiet now."

Below, something moved at the edge of the tree line.

Wren saw the shape of it before she could count them. Not one. Not four.

A loose line, spread wide, moving at a walk across the open ground toward the hut with a discipline that no rogue had ever had.

Six. She made it six.

They carried no torches, which struck her as the worst detail of all. Men who want to find something bring light. Men who already know where it is do not.

Two peeled off and went round the back of the hut without being told to.

One went to the door.

He did not knock, and he did not kick it. He put his hand flat against the wood, high up, and held it there, and Wren realized he was feeling for warmth through the door.

"They've done this before," she breathed.

"Every one of them."

The door came open. There was no shout, no scuffle, nothing, and the absence of noise was somehow worse than noise would have been.

A light went up inside. It moved room to room, which took no time at all, because there was only the one room.

Then it came out again.

The man who had felt the door crouched at the hearth for a long moment with his hand in the dead ashes.

Even at that distance, even in the dark, Wren saw him take his hand out and rub his fingers together and stand up, and she knew exactly what he had found, which was nothing, and exactly what it told him, which was also nothing.

"Cold hearth," Tamsin said, with no satisfaction in it at all.

The six of them stood in the snow outside the hut and did not search further.

That was what frightened her. They did not fan out into the trees or start a sweep or call to one another. They stood, briefly, the way men stand when a thing has been ticked off a list, and then they formed up and went back the way they had come.

"They're leaving."

"They're going on to the next one," Tamsin said. "There'll be a next one. They'll do six or eight tonight."

"Six or eight *what*?"

"Exiles. Registered ones, mostly. Anyone the Spire has reason to think is still breathing when the paperwork says otherwise."

Wren watched the line of them thin out across the snow and disappear into the trees, unhurried, a chore being completed.

Nobody down there had been angry. Nobody had been hunting. It had been a man putting his palm on a door to check the temperature, and six sets of boots walking away from an empty building, and a list getting shorter.

"They'll come back," she said.

"In a month or two. They always do." Tamsin finally sat, all at once, as if her legs had been waiting for permission.

"That's the rhythm of it. You learn it and you live around it."

Wren stayed crouched, looking down at the hut.

Something about it would not settle. Six men, no torches, no search. The whole thing had been too easy and had finished too neatly, and she could not make herself believe she had just watched the entire attempt.

"Tamsin," she said slowly. "If they only send six men to tick a list. What do they send when they actually want to find someone?"

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment.

"One," she said. "They send one."
