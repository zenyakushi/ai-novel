# Chapter 6: Left for the Wolves

\[Wren's POV\]

She walked twenty paces into the Wilds before she let herself understand what she had heard.

Then she did not walk. She ran.

The ground went down almost at once, scrub and loose stone under a skin of new snow, and her feet found none of it in the right order. Branches took skin off her forearms. She did not slow for them.

Behind her the shouting started, and it was not the shouting of men surprised.

They had been waiting for her to run. It made the rest of it easier for them, she understood, distantly, the way she understood the cold.

A body found downhill of the border had run. A body found at the post had been put there. Two different files, and only one of them got read twice.

Her lungs had gone to knives by the fourth breath. Nineteen years of keeping her head down had not built anything in her that was good for this.

The first one caught her at the bottom of the slope.

He did not tackle her. He simply arrived in front of her, the way a wall arrives, and her own speed did most of the work.

Wren went down hard on her shoulder and came up with a fistful of snow and grit, and threw it, because it was what her hand had in it.

It bought her a second. She spent it getting her feet under her.

"Kneel."

The word came out of the lead enforcer with everything behind it, the low command shape that went under the ribs and pulled. She had felt it twice in her life, both times from her own Alpha, and both times her knees had folded before she had decided anything.

Her knees did not fold.

She stood in the snow with her heart going and her breath tearing and nothing in her legs answering him at all.

Some part of her was more frightened by that than by the four of them. A command took you or it did not, and it had always taken her, and she had never once been asked whether she wanted it to.

The enforcer's face did something she would think about for a long time afterward. He said it again, harder, and the word broke over her and ran off her the way water runs off oilcloth.

"Again," said the tall one, and there was something new underneath his voice.

"I have said it twice."

"Then say it like you mean it."

"I did."

Nobody moved for the length of a breath. Four grown men and a branded girl in a shift, and the only thing any of them could hear was how quiet it had gone.

Wren ran.

She got nine paces. The tall one took her legs out from under her and the ground came up and took the rest.

Something opened along her ribs, warm and immediate, and she did not look, because looking was a thing you could do later.

Hands closed on her ankle. She turned into it instead of away, which surprised her, and drove her heel up into a face she could not see.

Cartilage gave. A man swore, and the grip came off her.

She had never hit anything in her life.

That was the part that would not sit right, even then, even with her mouth full of snow. Not that it had worked. That her body had chosen it without asking her, and had known where the face would be.

Nineteen years of making herself small, and something underneath all of it had been keeping its own counsel the whole time.

She was crawling before she knew she had started, elbows and knees, up the slope she had just come down, because down was where they expected her.

The split boulder was somewhere above her in the dark. She had counted it four hours ago for a walk back she was never going to take, and now it was the only fixed thing in the world, and she went toward it.

The brand dragged against the cold and the second pulse hammered under it, faster now, out of step with everything else in her chest.

Someone caught her hair.

Her head went back. The night sky swung across her vision, white with snow, and she got one hand up and raked at the arm and felt her nails find skin.

"Hold her."

"I am holding her."

"You are not."

She bit down on whatever was nearest and tasted wool and then blood, and the arm tore away from her, and for one absurd half second she was free again and moving.

The blow caught her at the back of the skull.

The snow came up very slowly this time. She had time to see it coming and no way to do anything about it, and then it was against her cheek, and it was not cold anymore, which she knew was wrong.

Boots crunched somewhere above her. She could hear them talking and could no longer sort the words into an order.

One of them laughed at something. Another told him to check her, and nobody did, and after a moment the first one said she was not going anywhere, and that settled it.

Her ribs were wet. The warmth was spreading down into the snow under her and she could feel exactly how far it had gone, which was further than she wanted it to be.

Her fingers were still curled around a handful of grit she did not remember picking up.

Stay unremarkable, her mother used to say.

Wren lay in the dark at the bottom of a country with no law in it, and could not remember the last time she had managed anything else.

The boots went away.

She listened to them go for a long time, longer than made sense, until the sound thinned out into the wind and she could not tell which was which.

The snow kept coming down on her face and stopped melting.

Under everything, slower than her heart and steadier, the second pulse went on beating.
