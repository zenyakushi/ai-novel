# Chapter 20: Seen

\[Wren's POV\]

Tamsin went up to the ridge alone first, and made Wren wait at the bottom, and came back down with her mouth in a line.

"Come and look. Don't walk on them."

They crouched at the edge of the disturbed patch. The light was flat and the prints held their shape well in the crust.

"Tell me what you see," Tamsin said.

"One man."

"Good. What else."

Wren made herself slow down.

"He came in from the east along the rock." She traced the line without touching it.

"He walked on stone as long as he could. He only crossed the open snow where there wasn't another way."

"Which tells you?"

"That he didn't want to leave a line."

"And he left one anyway, because there's no route up here that doesn't cross that gap." Tamsin nodded slowly.

"So he knew what he was doing, and he did it anyway, which means he wanted the position more than he wanted to be invisible. What else."

Wren looked at the long trough where the body had lain.

"He was here a while."

"Since before we came up. He watched you do the whole of it." Tamsin's voice had gone flat.

"Now the part that matters. Look at the boot."

Wren looked.

The print was deep at the heel and the tread was cut in a broad chevron, worn almost smooth at the ball of the foot, with a notch missing from the outer edge where something had taken a bite out of the sole.

She sat back on her heels.

"It's mended," she said. "He's had it stitched. You can see where the sole's been put back on."

"Sanctum boots are not mended." Tamsin stood up.

"They're issued, and when they go, you draw another pair from stores, and that's the end of it. A Sanctum tracker does not walk around on a sole somebody has sewn back on with a saddler's stitch."

Wren stayed crouched a moment longer.

Something reorganized itself in her head, unpleasantly, like a floor shifting.

Since the platform she had understood the world as having exactly one shape. There was the Sanctum, and the Sanctum wanted her dead, and everything that happened to her from now until the end would be some version of that.

It had been a terrible thing to believe. It had also been simple, and she had not noticed how much she had been leaning on the simplicity.

Her chest had gone tight around the loss of it. One enemy could be understood. One enemy had a shape, and a building, and a set of habits Tamsin had spent nineteen years learning.

Two meant the country she had landed in was not a place with a danger in it. It was a place made of them.

"He's not theirs," she said.

"No."

"Then whose?"

"That," said Tamsin, "is a much worse question, and we're going to have to answer it."

She started back down the slope.

"Pack up. Properly, this time. We're not coming back to that hut."

Wren did not move for a moment.

She had been in that hut six weeks. It was a turf roof and one room and a door with three bars, and she had bled on its floor and learned to split wood outside it, and she was surprised by how much it cost to leave.

Then she went down after Tamsin, because the alternative was standing on a ridge where a stranger had lain all morning watching her.

\[Scout's POV\]

He ran the first two miles because he could not help it, and then made himself slow down, because arriving out of breath would cost him the telling.

It was three hours to the camp at the old quarry. He used all of them putting it in order.

He had lain on that ridge since before first light. He had watched an old woman and a young one walk out into the open and sit down in the snow for the better part of an hour doing nothing that made any sense at all.

And then.

He still did not have a word for it, and he had spent three hours hunting one.

It had not been a light or a sound. He had felt it the way you feel someone come into a room behind you, except the room had been four hundred paces of open snow, and the thing that came into it had been much larger than a person.

His hands had gone to the rock and he had pressed himself flat, and he had lain there with his face in the crust and his heart going like a bird, and he was not a man who frightened easily. He had run the border eleven years.

Then it had stopped, and the girl had fallen over, and the old woman had gone to her, and the two of them had walked back down toward the tree line like nothing at all had happened.

The camp came up out of the dark as a smell before it was a light. Woodsmoke and horses.

They knew him at the picket and let him through without making him say it twice, which in itself told him his face was doing something.

"He's eating," the picket said.

"He'll want this."

"They all say that."

"Not like this."

Something in it got through. The man looked at him properly, and then jerked his chin toward the long tent at the quarry wall.

Inside, there was a brazier, and a map weighted at four corners with stones, and a man standing over it who did not look up when the flap came back.

"You've come off the eastern line four days early," he said.

"Yes."

"Then either you've been seen, or you've seen something."

"The second."

Now he did look up, and the scout, who had made this report to this man nine times in three years and had never once been nervous, found that his mouth had gone dry.

"Go on."

He went on. He gave it in the order he had built on the run, and he did not decorate any of it, and when he got to the part he had no word for he said so plainly and did not try.

The man at the map listened without moving. He did not interrupt once, which was worse than being interrupted, because it meant every word was going somewhere and being kept.

When it was done he stood for a while with his knuckles on the table.

"Say the part you have no word for again."

The scout said it again. It came out no better the second time.

"You lay down."

"I did not decide to, Alpha."

"No." Something moved behind the man's eyes. "I don't imagine you did."

"An old woman," he said, "and a girl."

"Yes, Alpha."

"How old is the girl?"

"Nineteen. Twenty."

"And she has a mark on her." It was not a question.

"On the collarbone," he went on. "Fresh."

The scout stared at him. "I was four hundred paces out, Alpha. I couldn't see her collarbone."

"No," Cassian Thorne said. "But I'd have wagered the tent on it."

He straightened up from the map, and he was smiling slightly, and it was not a pleasant thing to watch.

"They branded a girl at Duskmoor six weeks ago and put her over the border to die," he said. "It was the talk of four packs and then it was the talk of none, which is the part that always interests me."

He looked back down at the map and put one finger on the empty country east of the quarry, where nothing was drawn because nobody had ever thought it worth the ink.

"Thornhollow has been waiting four generations for the Sanctum to make a mistake," he said.

"Not a cruelty. They are never short of those. A mistake."

"And she did not die," Cassian said. "She sat down in the snow and made a grown tracker lie on his face."
