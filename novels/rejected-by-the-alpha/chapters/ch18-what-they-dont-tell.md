# Chapter 18: What They Don't Tell

\[Wren's POV\]

"I was told this once," Tamsin said, "by a woman who was dying, and I did not believe a word of it for eleven years."

"The girl in the store room."

"Her grandmother's telling, passed down. She gave it to me in pieces over two days because she could not stay awake for longer than a few minutes at a time." Tamsin had not turned round from the fire.

"So it comes to you in pieces. That's not me withholding. That's all there is."

"Tell me the pieces."

"Sit closer. I'm not saying this loudly."

Wren moved to the floor beside the hearth. Her heart had begun going harder than the work required.

"Before the bond," Tamsin said, "there was a line that did not need it."

She let that sit.

"That's the first piece and it's the one everything else hangs off. There were wolves who could reach the moon directly."

"No rite, no officiant, no thread handed down by anybody. They simply had it."

"Had what?"

"That's not in the pieces." Tamsin's mouth twisted.

"She did not know. Her grandmother did not know. Whatever it was, it was old enough that what survived was the fact of it and not the shape."

Wren's hands had gone tight in her lap.

"And the other houses?"

"The other houses could not do it. That's the second piece." Tamsin turned round at last and sat down with her back to the hearth.

"Imagine what that's worth. Every pack in the world takes its legitimacy from the moon, and there is one bloodline that can reach it without going through anybody."

"Then they'd rule."

"They didn't, as it happens. The telling says they were keepers, not rulers. They held the connection and they did not use it to take anything."

Tamsin's voice went dry. "Make of that what you like. It didn't help them."

"What happened."

"The other houses built something."

The fire cracked. Wren did not move.

"They built a thing that would do what the line did naturally," Tamsin said. "Except it wouldn't be in the blood. It would be in a rite."

"And a rite needs an officiant, and an officiant answers to someone, and someone keeps the records."

"The bond."

"The bond."

Wren heard her own breath go out.

"It's made?"

"That's the third piece and it's the one that took me eleven years." Tamsin leaned her head back against the stone.

"Everything about it tells you so, once you'll let yourself look. It has a procedure. It has an appeal."

"It has an exception. It has a form of words for cancelling it, and a mark for enforcing the cancellation."

"Gods don't write procedure."

"Gods don't write procedure," Tamsin agreed. "Councils do."

Wren sat very still on the floor of a turf hut nine miles past the border, with the black lines cold on her collarbone, and felt something enormous and slow turn over underneath her whole life.

"Why," she said. "If they just wanted the thing for themselves. Why build a copy at all."

"Because a copy that everybody has to come to is better than an original that one family is born with." Tamsin's eyes were steady on her. "And because of what it does that the original didn't."

"Which is?"

"It shows them who's got it."

The fire moved.

"Every unmated wolf in the Dominion walks onto that platform at the same age," Tamsin said. "Every single one, by law, in front of witnesses, where the Sanctum can see. Tell me what that is, if it isn't a gift."

Wren's mouth had gone dry.

"It's a net," she said.

"It's a net. It has been a net for four hundred years, and it is cast once a season, in every pack, and nobody has ever once called it that, because it comes with a hymn."

"Then the Rite isn't for finding mates."

"The Rite finds mates. That's what makes it good." Tamsin's voice had no pity in it.

"It works. Half the wolves you grew up with will die beside someone that ceremony found them, and be glad of it, and none of that is a lie. It's simply not what it's for."

Wren put her palm flat on the floor because the room had tilted slightly.

"My mother made me hide," she said. "From four years old. Before I could have told anyone anything."

"Yes."

"She'd have had to know what the Rite was. Not suspect. Know."

"Yes," Tamsin said again, and then, after a moment: "You understand what that means about her."

Wren did. She turned away from it, because it was too big and too new and it would keep.

"The line," she said instead. "The one that didn't need it. What happened to them."

Tamsin was quiet for a while.

"What happens to anyone the net is for."

"Say it."

"They were hunted." Tamsin's voice had dropped.

"Not all at once. That's the fourth piece, and it's the ugliest one, because it wasn't a war. It was four hundred years of ceremonies, and quiet dispositions, and children who were flagged before they could walk and dealt with when they came of age."

"That's not possible. Somebody would remember."

"Somebody did. She died in a store room and I did not believe her." Tamsin's hands were flat on her knees.

"You've been to a Rite every year of your life. Did you ever once count how many came back down those steps?"

Wren could not answer her.

"Nor did anyone," Tamsin said.

The wind had got up outside. Wren listened to it and found that her hands had stopped shaking, which surprised her, and that the second pulse under her breastbone had not altered at all, which did not.

"They had a name," she said. It was not a question.

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment.

"You said it yourself," she said. "On the second night, in the fever, in a voice that was not the one you're using now. I have not slept a whole night since."

"Say it anyway."

Tamsin leaned forward, and the firelight went up the side of her face, and she said it quietly, the way you say a word that has cost people their lives.

"Emberveil."
