# Chapter 5: The Long Walk

\[Wren's POV\]

Garrick's hand stayed on her arm the whole way through the lower court, and not once did he tighten it.

Wren counted that. It was a small thing, and she had nothing bigger left to hold.

Behind them the amphitheater emptied in a slow shuffling tide. Eight hundred wolves had watched the thread catch between her and their Alpha. Not one of them walked beside her now.

The cold found her before the gate did. Her ceremony shift had been cut for standing still under torchlight, and the stone under her bare feet had been holding the night since dusk.

Her hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the last corridor. She noticed it the way a person notices a sound that has stopped.

She took the next step anyway. Then the one after it.

Duskmoor at this hour should have been shuttered and dark. It wasn't. Lamps burned behind half the windows on the mill road, and every one of them went out as she came level with it.

That was the part she had not braced for. Not shouting. The tidiness of it.

A door closed on her left, careful and slow, the way you shut a door when you would rather nobody heard you shut it. Then a second. Then a third, further up the road, before she had even reached it.

Word moved faster than she did.

"Eyes ahead," Garrick said, barely moving his mouth.

She kept them ahead.

At the baker's corner a woman stood in her doorway with a child on her hip. Wren knew her. Mira had sold her yesterday's bread at half price for three winters, and had once waited out a rainstorm to return a coin Wren had left on the counter.

Mira turned the child's face into her shoulder and stepped back inside.

The brand under Wren's collarbone had stopped burning somewhere around the second door. She found she wanted it back. The pain had at least belonged to her.

The outer gate stood open, which it never did at night.

Four Sanctum enforcers waited beneath it in grey, and the torches set behind them made a wall of light she had to walk into to be seen.

Garrick stopped three paces short.

"She reaches the border," he said.

"We have the order." The lead enforcer did not look at him. He looked at her collarbone, the way the visiting Alphas had.

Garrick's fingers stayed where they were a moment past the point where they should have opened.

"Garrick." The enforcer put out a hand. Not impatient, only certain.

His grip let go. Cold moved into the place where his hand had been, and her whole arm reported it at once, as though the warmth had been the only thing keeping the night off her.

"Wren."

He said it low, and it was the first time since the platform that anyone had used her name instead of her bloodline. Her throat closed around a response she did not get to make.

"Once you are through, keep moving. Do not stop to rest, whatever else you do."

She turned to look at him. He was already looking at the ground.

"Is that Kieran's message, or yours?"

His jaw worked. He did not answer, and the enforcer's hand closed around her arm before he could decide to.

They walked faster than Garrick had.

The road past the gate stopped being a road within a mile, and after that it was frozen cart ruts and then it was nothing, only the pale line where feet had gone before hers. Snow started somewhere in the second mile, fine and dry, more like sand than weather.

Nobody spoke. The enforcers had the easy silence of men doing a errand they had done before.

Her feet stopped reporting back to her somewhere past the third mile. She thought about that instead of thinking about the rest, and it worked for a while.

Under her ribs, the second pulse kept its own time, slower than the one in her wrists and steadier than it had any business being out here. She had spent nineteen years pretending not to feel it. It was harder tonight, with so little else left in her to listen to.

Then she began counting.

Not steps. She had given up on steps. She counted the things she would need if she ever came back along this road: the split boulder where the track bent north, the dead pine with the lightning scar down its face, the point where the sound of the river dropped away behind the ridge and did not come back.

Nobody had told her she was permitted to think about coming back.

Nobody had told her she was not.

She catalogued four more before the ridge, and each one settled somewhere behind her breastbone and stayed there.

Her chest had been hollow since the platform. This was the first thing that had gone into it since.

The last checkpoint was a low post of grey stone with the Dominion's mark cut into the face of it, and a brazier beside it that had gone out long enough ago to have snow in the ashes.

One of the enforcers put a hand flat on the post as he passed, the way a man touches a doorframe on his way out of a house.

Past it, the ground fell away into scrub and black trees, and the snow came off the open ground sideways.

The Wilds did not look like anything. That was the part nobody had ever said about them.

She had grown up on stories where the border was a wall, or a wound, and it was neither. It was only more country, going on, with nobody's law laid over it.

Her eyes kept trying to find an edge out there and kept coming back with nothing.

"Far enough," the lead enforcer said.

Wren stopped and turned.

He was not looking at her. He had drawn the other three in close around him, heads down, and his voice had dropped into the register men use when a thing has already been decided and they are only settling the shape of it.

The wind took most of it. She caught three words whole.

"Before first light."

The tall one's eyes came up to her and went away again. "And if she is found?"

"The Wilds took her." The lead enforcer said it flatly, like a line he had been handed rather than one he had thought of. "That is all that goes in the file."
